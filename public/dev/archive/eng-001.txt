Marker: MARIE CURIE – A LIFE OF SCIENTIFIC DEVOTION
Born Maria Sklodowska in Warsaw, Poland, in 1867, Marie Curie grew up in a patriotic family of teachers who valued education above all else. [I] During her youth, Poland was under foreign rule, and women were strictly barred from enrolling in universities. [II] Determined to pursue higher education, she worked as a governess for several years to finance her sister Bronislawa's medical studies in Paris, with the agreement that her sister would later help her in return. [III] In 1891, Marie finally moved to France and enrolled at the Sorbonne, where she lived in a freezing garret and survived on bread and tea while dedicating every waking hour to physics and mathematics. [IV]
In 1894, she met Pierre Curie, a fellow physicist who shared her intense passion for scientific inquiry. Their marriage marked the beginning of a remarkable partnership that transformed modern science. Working in a leaky, poorly ventilated shed without financial support, the couple processed tons of pitchblende ore by hand. Their relentless perseverance led to the discovery of two new radioactive elements, polonium—named after Marie's beloved homeland—and radium. In 1903, Marie became the first woman in history to be awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, sharing the honor with Pierre and Henri Becquerel.
Tragedy struck in 1906 when Pierre was killed in a street accident in Paris. Devastated yet unbroken, Marie took over his teaching post, becoming the first female professor at the Sorbonne. Despite facing severe gender prejudice from the academic establishment, she continued her groundbreaking research and won a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911. Crucially, she refused to patent the radium-isolation process so that the global scientific community could research it freely without financial barriers.
When World War I broke out in 1914, Curie suspended her laboratory work to bring medical technology directly to the battlefield. Recognizing that prompt X-ray examinations could save soldiers' lives, she developed mobile radiography units, affectionately known as 'petites Curies.' She even learned to drive and repair vehicles herself while training over a hundred women as X-ray technicians. Years of unprotected exposure to radiation eventually led to aplastic anemia, and she passed away in 1934, leaving behind an indelible legacy of genius and humanitarian devotion.

Marker: TRADITIONAL FESTIVALS IN THE MODERN ERA
Across the globe, traditional festivals have long served as living anchors of community memory and cultural identity. Whether it is the vibrant lantern parades of the Mid-Autumn Festival in Asia, the colorful Día de los Muertos in Mexico, or the lively Highland Games in Scotland, these annual celebrations pass down moral values, folklore, and artistic skills from one generation to the next. By participating in shared rituals and folk games like tug of war or bamboo dancing, individuals strengthen their sense of belonging.
In recent decades, the reach of many local festivities has expanded dramatically beyond national borders. [I] Thanks to global media and international tourism, holidays such as Halloween, Diwali, and Lunar New Year are now celebrated by diverse populations worldwide. [II] In many Vietnamese schools, for instance, students eagerly dress up for Halloween while also preparing traditional five-fruit trays during Tet. [III] This cross-cultural participation allows young people to experience global festivities firsthand and cultivate tolerance toward unfamiliar customs. [IV]
However, the growing popularity of traditional festivals has also brought the challenge of over-commercialization. In many tourist destinations, sacred ceremonies are shortened or staged artificially to entertain visitors on tight schedules. Furthermore, heavy advertising often shifts the focus of holidays from family reunion and spiritual reflection to compulsive shopping. When young people view festivals merely as occasions to buy trendy costumes or post photos online, they risk losing touch with the historical origins of the celebrations.
To keep festivals both vibrant and meaningful, communities are finding innovative ways to balance modernization with heritage preservation. Instead of rejecting contemporary trends altogether, cultural organizers use digital platforms and interactive workshops to explain the deeper symbolism behind ancient rituals. When youths understand why an ancestral custom matters, they become active guardians of their heritage rather than passive consumers in a globalized world.

Marker: THE ENVIRONMENTAL COST OF FAST FASHION
Over the past twenty years, the global apparel industry has undergone a dramatic transformation driven by 'fast fashion'—a business model that mass-produces cheap, trendy clothing at breakneck speed. Whereas fashion brands once released four seasonal collections a year, many now introduce new styles almost weekly to satisfy consumers' appetite for novelty. Because these garments are sold at rock-bottom prices, shoppers frequently treat clothes as single-use items, wearing them only a few times before throwing them away.
Behind the glittering shop windows, however, lies a staggering environmental toll. [I] The fashion sector accounts for nearly ten percent of global carbon emissions—more than international flights and maritime shipping combined. [II] Producing a single cotton shirt requires roughly 2,700 liters of fresh water, enough for one person to drink for two and a half years. [III] Furthermore, dyeing and finishing fabrics involve toxic chemicals that are often discharged untreated into rivers, severely contaminating local water supplies. [IV]
The crisis deepens when unwanted garments are discarded. Over sixty percent of modern clothing is made from synthetic fibers such as polyester, which is derived from fossil fuels. When these clothes are dumped into landfills, they can take up to two hundred years to decompose. Even during regular household washing, synthetic garments shed millions of microscopic plastic fibers that passed through sewage treatment plants and enter marine food chains.
Fortunately, growing environmental awareness is inspiring a shift toward sustainable fashion. By choosing durable clothing, swapping outfits with friends, and supporting brands that use recycled materials, consumers can significantly shrink their fashion carbon footprint. Charity shops and online second-hand platforms have also gained immense popularity among eco-conscious youths. Ultimately, adopting a greener wardrobe is not about sacrificing personal style; rather, it is a vital commitment to protecting the planet in the long run.
